Overview

When people need help, getting it shouldn't be the hard part

Faveway is a concept iOS app that connects people who need assistance — from grocery runs to emergency response — with nearby providers. As Lead Designer and Project Manager, I owned the end-to-end design: research, IA, prototyping, and testing.

  • Identified emotional and logistical barriers preventing people from seeking help
  • Designed a trust-first experience that makes reaching out to strangers feel safe
  • Built emergency access as a core pillar — reachable in one tap from anywhere

Empathise

The barrier isn't access — it's hesitation

Through user interviews and observational research, I found that people delay asking for help not just because of limited resources, but due to social discomfort and distrust. In emergencies, this hesitation becomes dangerous.

People struggle to connect with others for assistance — whether for daily needs or emergencies — due to hesitation, distrust, and lack of accessible channels.

I synthesised findings via affinity mapping in FigJam, aligning the team around a core insight: the barriers are emotional as much as logistical.

Identified opportunities

Three design opportunities emerged from research

After synthesising research insights, I identified three key opportunity areas that shaped the design direction of Faveway:

Reducing barriers to asking for help

Hesitation and distrust were the biggest blockers. I designed trust signals — ratings, verified profiles, and transparent pricing — to make it feel safe to reach out to a stranger.

Streamlining emergency access

GPS-enabled matching and a one-tap emergency button were key design decisions to reduce friction in high-stress moments, where every second and every tap counts.

Creating mutual value

The platform needed to feel worthwhile for both sides. I designed the provider experience to be simple and income-generating, encouraging participation from those with available skills and time.

User personas

Designing for those who need help the most

Based on my research, I developed two primary personas representing the core user group — people who regularly face challenges that require outside assistance but don't have a reliable way to access it.

JL

Jane Leviste

Housewife, 32 · Manila

Full-time homemaker with 3 kids. Manages household weekdays and dedicates weekends to family. Handles weekly groceries every Friday — but a mild scoliosis diagnosis means lifting is becoming harder to manage alone.

Goals
  • Complete errands independently without physical strain
  • Find trustworthy, nearby help quickly when needed
  • Feel safe letting someone new into her routine
Frustrations
  • Mild scoliosis limits her ability to lift heavy items
  • Problems with balance and coordination on busy days
  • No reliable platform to find vetted, casual help nearby
MJ

Maria Juan

Retired Teacher, 65 · Manila

Widow living alone with 2 adult children working abroad. Enjoys local TV and gardening. Fiercely independent — she doesn't want to burden her kids, but health concerns are making everyday tasks harder to manage.

Goals
  • Maintain independence at home without burdening family
  • Access reliable help for house maintenance and health emergencies
Frustrations
  • Can't do heavy cleaning or maintenance tasks safely
  • Occasional dizziness makes emergency situations unpredictable
  • Doesn't know who to call in an emergency — and fears being ignored

Ideation

Starting from empathy, designing for action

I led a collaborative FigJam ideation session using How Might We questions. The team voted on ideas and grouped them by feasibility and impact — moving quickly from concepts to testable flows.

A critical decision: treat the emergency feature as a core pillar, not a secondary feature. This shifted the entire IA — emergency access needed to be one tap from anywhere in the app.

How might we connect users with the exact help they need — without overwhelming them with choices?
How might we overcome the discomfort users feel when asking strangers for help?
How might we create income opportunities for providers using their available skills and time?

Usability testing

Validating with real users in real scenarios

I conducted unmoderated usability testing through Maze with participants who matched our target profiles — smartphone users of varying ages and tech literacy levels. The test scenario was intentionally broad to allow organic exploration of the app.

Scenario: You need someone to assist or help you. As a first-time Faveway user, explore how this product will help solve your daily needs and problems in completing your task.

Reflections

What I learned — and what I'd do differently

Faveway taught me that designing for trust is harder than designing a flow. You can't just add a rating system and call it done — every micro-decision either builds or erodes confidence.

  • The 80% booking success rate flagged friction in the confirmation flow — I iterated post-testing by reducing steps from four to two
  • I'd invest more in the provider-side experience, which was under-explored in this version
  • I'd run a dedicated accessibility test with older users — Maria's persona deserved more validation

Leading this project end-to-end sharpened my ability to balance research rigour with speed — knowing when to go deeper versus when to prototype and test quickly.
