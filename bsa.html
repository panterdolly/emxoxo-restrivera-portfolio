Overview

When users don't understand why — they don't engage

Voila is an English online platform developed by Cambridge University Press & Assessment for non-native speakers at a CEFR level of B1–B2. It helps learners improve their English skills through short, accessible activities.

As Lead UI/UX Designer, I was tasked with redesigning the activity homepage — specifically addressing a transparency problem: learners were being shown recommended activities after completing an anchor test, but had no understanding of why those activities were recommended for them.

  • Applied the concept of "labor perception bias" to explain recommendations and build trust
  • Redesigned the activity homepage to improve clarity, transparency, and first-activity engagement
  • Delivered measurable improvements — 80% task comprehension and increased activity completion rates

Empathise

Recommended — but why?

After learners completed the anchor test and viewed their results, they were directed to the activity homepage showing recommended activities. The problem was clear: there was no explanation of why these activities were selected for them specifically.

This lack of transparency undermined user trust and reduced engagement. Learners who don't understand the logic behind a recommendation are far less likely to act on it — especially in an educational context where trust in the system directly impacts motivation.

Users directed to the activity homepage after their anchor test see recommended activities — but with no transparency on why these activities were selected for them.

The solution needed to enhance transparency, increase user trust, and improve engagement — all without adding complexity to an already multi-step flow.

Opportunity

Trust is built when users see the work behind the result

By applying the concept of labor perception bias — the idea that people trust and value things more when they can see the underlying work or reasoning behind them — I saw an opportunity to redesign the recommendation experience in a way that made the system feel fair, transparent, and personal.

Explain the recommendation

Show learners why each activity was recommended based on their anchor test results — making the system feel intelligent and personalised, not arbitrary.

Increase first-activity engagement

Reduce the number of learners who drop off before starting their first activity by making the purpose and value of each recommendation immediately clear.

Build long-term trust

Continuous research and user feedback would further refine the recommendation process — creating a system that improves with use and grows with the learner.

User personas

Two learners, two different barriers to engagement

The research surfaced two distinct learner profiles — a young student seeking clarity and simplicity, and an older student frustrated by confusing UI patterns and unclear feedback.

SD

Sally Doe

Student, 11 · Full-time student

A diligent student on a journey to practice English. A bookworm at heart, Sally uses YouTube and reading to build her skills — but gets frustrated when learning platforms put too many steps between her and the content.

Goals
  • Optimised, straightforward learning material with clear feedback
  • Get to reading and listening activities quickly without unnecessary steps
Frustrations
  • Too long a process before getting into the actual learning material
  • Unclear navigation and feedback slows her down
MD

Milan Dimitri

Student, 18 · Full-time student

A fan of fan fiction and gaming, Milan expects digital interfaces to be intuitive and rewarding. He gets frustrated when UI elements are confusing or when feedback doesn't feel accurate or meaningful.

Goals
  • Straightforward learning material with clear, accurate feedback
  • Icons and UI elements that are intuitive and purposeful
Frustrations
  • Complex and confusing learning material and UI elements
  • Dull feedback and confusing iconography

Ideation

Designing for clarity and trust from the first interaction

The ideation phase was guided by two focused How Might We questions that addressed both the transparency problem and the engagement drop-off:

How might we improve the clarity of the purpose of recommended activities for learners?
How might we increase the number of learners who take their first activity?

All design decisions were made within the Cambridge One Design System and branding guidelines — ensuring consistency with the broader Cambridge platform ecosystem while solving the specific transparency challenge.

Reflections

People trust what they can see the work behind

The biggest takeaway from this project was a fundamental insight about human psychology: users trust and value things more when they see the underlying work behind the result or process. This is the essence of labor perception bias — and designing for it changed how I think about transparency in product design.

  • Explaining the "why" behind a recommendation is as important as the recommendation itself
  • Working within Cambridge One Design System constraints pushed me to solve problems more creatively within guardrails
  • The 80% comprehension rate validated the approach — but the 20% gap pointed to areas for further iteration
  • Faster task completion time (129.5s avg) showed that transparency doesn't have to come at the cost of speed
